# Three lenses: designer, client, brand

Every review reads the work through three lenses, in this order. The brand lens is the last one because it is the easiest to check and the least likely to find what matters: a page can match every token and still confuse the person holding it.

The client lens is an informed proxy, not user research. Say so in the review. For anything with real money or reputation at stake (a seller package, a new listing sheet going out to strangers), recommend that one or two people outside the team read it cold before it ships.

## Who reads what we make

These are working roles from `PRODUCT.md`, not real people. Never fill them with invented names, incomes, ages or quotes.

| Reader | What they hold | What they need from it |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer (NJ, NYC or FL) | A buyer package, a listing sheet, a neighbourhood or town guide | To understand the market, what the process costs, what happens next, and who to call |
| Seller | A seller pitch, CMA, brochure or marketing piece | Why this price or this approach, what they get, what it will cost them, whether to trust the agent |
| Prospect (met at a showing, an open house or a referral) | A showsheet, brochure, or the NJ footprint | A fast read: what this is, whether it is for them, why this team |
| Team member (teammate or agent) | A tool they run to make a print | The tool to be fast to use, predictable, and not to lose their work |
| Matt (owner) | Everything | Work that is correct, on brand, and safe to send |

For each output, name the reader before you judge it. A seller pitch and a buyer package have different jobs.

## Lens 1: designer

Judge the piece as a skilled graphic designer would, against its own purpose. Check each item and record the ones that fail, with the location.

**Purpose and hierarchy**
- Can you say what the piece is for in one sentence after three seconds? If not, the hierarchy is wrong.
- Is there one primary element on each page or screen? Is it the thing the reader most needs?
- Do the sizes, weights and colours step down in a clear order: title, section, body, caption, label?
- Does the eye move in the order the content is meant to be read, page by page and top to bottom?

**Grid, space and rhythm**
- Do the margins match on each edge and each page of the same piece?
- Do items that look aligned share an actual edge, baseline or column?
- Is the white space deliberate? Flag orphaned lines, a heading stranded at a page foot, a half-empty page, a block crammed against an edge.
- Do repeated items (agent cards, town rows, listing tiles) space identically, without a last-row stub that looks like an accident?

**Type craft**
- Line length: roughly 45–85 characters for running text.
- Leading loose enough to read, tight enough to hold together. Headings tighter than body.
- Label caps are tracked and small, not shouting; no more than two type families on a page, and the family follows the surface rules.
- Numbers in a column align on the decimal or the right edge.
- No widows or orphans that a designer would fix by hand.

**Images as design**
- Does the photo carry the page, or is it decoration? Is the crop deliberate (the subject, the horizon, a face) at both paper sizes?
- Are image edges, captions and credits consistent across the set?
- Is there one clear focal image per spread, not three competing?

**Colour as design**
- Is there a clear figure and ground? Navy on Paper reads; Sky or Cyan text on Paper does not.
- Does colour create emphasis, or is it everywhere? One accent is usually enough.
- In a set, do the pieces feel like a family without repeating each other exactly?

**Craft and finish**
- Rules, borders and hairlines: consistent weight, not some heavier than others.
- Corners, radii and shadows (on screens): one system, not a mix.
- Any element that looks like a placeholder. A stamp is fine when it is deliberate; a stray grey box is not.
- Motion (loops and screens): one move at a time, eased, nothing that flickers or bursts.

**The generic test**
- Would this look like a template anyone could have made? If yes, name the one or two specific choices that would make it this team's work (a scale contrast, a tracked caps label, a single bold shape, a proper Paper-ground piece). Keep it to two suggestions.

## Lens 2: client

Read the piece as the reader described above would, in the order they would encounter it. Use the physical print where one exists; do not read it on a large screen and call that the client's experience.

**Walkthrough (do it, and write down what you did)**
1. Hold it at arm's length (or view at 100% on the phone-width capture). Note the first thing your eye lands on, and whether it is what the piece wants you to see.
2. Read the headline and nothing else. Can you say what this is, who it is for, and what you are meant to do?
3. Read the first paragraph of the first body section. Is it plain? Does it answer the obvious question, or does it raise a new one?
4. Find the price, the fee, the figures, the next step, and the contact. Are they where a reader would look for them? Are they labelled so a non-specialist understands them without the agent in the room?
5. Turn each page. Note where you stop wanting to read, and where you get lost (page order, a missing back-reference, a term defined later).
6. Look for the one thing a worried reader would ask: is this honest, is it current, is it real? Check that every figure has a date or source, every name has a role, every claim has something behind it.
7. End on the last page. Is there a clear next step, and does the last page sign off the work?

**Clarity and trust**
- Plain words. Flag jargon a buyer or seller would not know (for example "net sheet", "DOM", "ARV", "co-op board package") unless the piece defines it on the page.
- Every acronym is spelled out on first use, or is familiar to the audience.
- Dates are shown and current (sold dates, listing dates, "updated" stamps). An old date with no label looks stale.
- Any claim that could be wrong is attributable: a source line, a date, or a plain qualification.
- No promises the team cannot keep (a timeline, a guaranteed price, "best in town") unless the source supports it.
- Placeholders and stamps: a reader who sees "List needed" or "Work in progress" on a page going to a client has seen an unfinished piece. Flag any stamp on a piece that is due to ship.

**Reading ease for the actual audience**
- Body text at print size is comfortable to read at arm's length for an older reader. Flag anything that needs squinting.
- Captions and footers are legible at print size, not just on screen.
- Contrast is strong enough in print (AA rules apply to print too). Light grey on white is a fail.
- On a phone, the first screen tells the reader what they are looking at, and the primary action is reachable with a thumb.

**Action and next step**
- Is there one obvious thing to do next? Two equal calls to action compete.
- Is the contact path correct, current and the team's own (never a placeholder number or an unchecked email)?
- Does the piece tell a buyer or seller what happens after they call, in a sentence?

**Respect and tone**
- Does it talk to the reader as a capable adult, without hype or condescension?
- Does it avoid any wording that reads as pressure, a hard sell to a seller who is anxious, or a promise to a buyer about an outcome?
- Does it name the agents and the team accurately, with the Douglas Elliman affiliation where the packages carry it.

**Fairness and inclusion**
- Does any photo, phrase or example assume a single kind of buyer or household? Flag it as a question for the team, not a rewrite.
- Are the people shown and named in the piece the people who work there (per the rule that copy and photos come from real GVC sources)?

**Would they keep it?**
- Is there anything a client would keep, pin up, or pass on? If nothing, say what would earn a second look without adding invented content.

## Lens 3: brand

Use `gvc-design-standards` and `references/review-checklist.md`. Brand is a checklist, not a feeling: colours and tokens, type faces, the Douglas Elliman and team boilerplate, voice (no taglines, no decorative eyebrows, no gradient text, no glass), image rules, stamps, market independence and frozen outputs.

Brand is also the consistency check across the set: does this piece look like it belongs next to the others the team has already put out? If it reads as a different company, say which rule it breaks, or say the rule itself has not been written down yet.

## Writing the three lenses into the report

- Designer findings: name the element and the failure ("the agent-card grid leaves a single orphan card on page 2"), and the fix in design terms.
- Client findings: name the reader and the moment ("a seller reading page 3 cannot tell what the 'net' figure includes").
- Brand findings: name the rule from the standards.
- Put the highest-impact client finding ahead of style nits. A client misreading a figure outranks a kerning issue.
- If the three lenses disagree (a design choice that is on brand but confusing for a buyer), report both and say which way you lean and why.
- Mark which findings come from the walkthrough and which from the checklist, so Matt can see what was actually read.
